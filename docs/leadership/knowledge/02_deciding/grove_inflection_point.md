# Grove and Moore — "what would our successors do?"

**Intel, 1985. Andy Grove (President) and Gordon Moore (CEO). Recounted by Grove in *Only the Paranoid Survive* (1996).**

## The situation

Intel was founded as a memory company and its identity was memory. By the mid-1980s Japanese
manufacturers were producing DRAM of higher quality at lower cost, and Intel's memory business had
been losing money for most of two years. Everyone could see the numbers. Nobody could act on them,
because exiting memory meant contradicting what the company believed itself to be — and a widely
held internal conviction that memory was the "technology driver" the rest of the business depended
on.

Meanwhile a side business in microprocessors was growing.

## What was done

Grove describes asking Moore, in Moore's office:

> "If we got kicked out and the board brought in a new CEO, what do you think he would do?"

Moore answered without hesitation: he would get us out of memories.

Grove's reply was to suggest they walk out of the door, come back in, and do it themselves.

The exit took over a year, cost jobs and was fought internally. Intel became a microprocessor
company.

## Why it worked

The question is a **debiasing device**, and a precise one. It strips out sunk cost, identity, and
loyalty to the past decision, and asks only: given the position on the board right now, what is the
best move? It works because it is easier to be honest about what a *stranger* should do than about
what you should do, and the answer is the same.

Grove's other contribution here is the concept of the **strategic inflection point**: the moment
when the fundamentals of a business change, which is visible in the data long before it is
actionable in the organisation. The gap between "we can see it" and "we can act on it" is where
companies die. Kodak could see digital. Nokia could see the smartphone.

## The principle

**When a decision is entangled with identity or sunk cost, ask what a competent stranger with no
history would do, and then take that action yourself.** The information is usually already in the
room; what is missing is permission.

## For us

Two applications, one comfortable and one not.

**The comfortable one.** We already ran this successfully. `LEADER_BIBLE.md` §4 records that the
`had_tal_move` metric was complexity with no material check, that a "the London is sharp" verdict
had been built on it, and that the *user* caught it. The response was not to defend the metric but
to rename it, ground themes in `docs/THEME_DEFINITIONS.md`, and keep Steer/Tal as an aim while
discarding the broken measurement. That is the stranger's answer.

**The uncomfortable one.** The 9,000-game profile regeneration is a memory business. It exists,
it has infrastructure, briefs, a Kaggle bundle and about fifty days of projected engine time — and
the stranger's question is whether a profile built from 8,617 bullet games is the thing that most
advances a job search with a permit expiring in about eighteen months. I do not think the answer is
obviously yes, and I have not asked it plainly before now.

The same question applies to this corpus. A stranger arriving today, told the interview is the live
item and the applicant is spending borrowed money, would ask what a hundred files about Shackleton
buy that an afternoon of interview drilling does not. I am writing it because it was asked for, and
I would be failing the job if I did not put the question on the page.
