# Marshall contradicting Roosevelt

**Washington, November 1938. George C. Marshall was then a brigadier general, deputy chief of staff — not yet Chief of Staff.**

## The situation

Roosevelt convened a White House meeting on rearmament and laid out a plan heavily weighted toward
aircraft production, with far less provision for the ground forces, ordnance and logistics that
would make those aircraft usable. He went round the room; the room agreed. He turned to Marshall,
the most junior man present, evidently expecting the same, and said something to the effect of
"Don't you think so, George?"

## What was done

Marshall's reply is well attested:

> "I am sorry, Mr. President, but I don't agree with that at all."

Two details matter. He objected on the substance — that aircraft without the supporting establishment
would not produce combat power — and he objected *in the room*, in front of everyone who had just
agreed. Accounts record that colleagues afterwards commiserated with him as a man whose career had
just ended.

Marshall was appointed Chief of Staff of the Army the following year.

## Why it worked

Not because dissent is inherently rewarded — it usually is not, and Marshall clearly expected it to
cost him. It worked because of what surrounded it.

He dissented on a matter within his professional competence, with a specific technical reason, once,
and then executed loyally. He had no record of habitual objection to spend. And he disliked being
called "George" by people entitled to call him that — the formality was part of the same character:
the relationship was professional, and professional judgement was the thing he was there to supply.

The deeper point is about the *questioner*. Roosevelt kept him and promoted him, which means the
system was capable of receiving the information. Dissent is a two-party act. A subordinate's courage
is worth nothing against a superior who punishes it, and most organisations discover this only in the
accident report.

## The principle

**Dissent is a duty when it concerns your professional competence, and it must be spent on
substance.** And its counterpart: **a leader who is never contradicted is not receiving
information.**
