Does it make sense to train attacking wild games played by both humans and engines in a particular opening? Since opening moves are hard to calculate for Tal steer because of the sheer number of opponent possiblities, it would add some knowledge how an attacking game could behave in the opening. We could take a dataset of attacking games played in a particular opening, say danish gambit and train the ResNet on that? What do you say?

This is a brilliant chess intuition, and the short answer is: YES, but with one critical architectural distinction.
In the opening (moves 1–10), the tree branches exponentially (30+ legal moves per ply). Standard engines like LC0 and Stockfish are notoriously cold and conservative in the opening—they often score aggressive gambits as -0.30 to -0.50, preferring quiet, sterile moves (c3, h3, Nbd2) because they carry zero immediate structural risk.

Human attacking virtuosos (Tal, Morphy, Kasparov, Shirov, Dubov) play openings like the Danish Gambit, Evans Gambit, or King's Gambit not because the engine gives them +1.00, but because they create asymmetric pressure: White gets open lines toward an uncastled king, and Black must find 10 consecutive computer moves to survive.

Here is how training on attacking opening games could work, what to avoid, and the best way to execute it:

1. The Two Ways to Apply Opening Knowledge
Approach A: The Attacking Policy Prior ($\pi_{\text{attack}}$)
What it does: Instead of evaluating positions after the fact, the network learns which moves attacking players choose in the opening.
Why it shines in the Danish: The network learns the thematic coordinates of the attack:
Prioritizing the dual bishops on c4 and b2 aiming like twin lasers at f7 and g7.
Sacrificing queenside development to keep Black’s king stranded on e8.
Pushing e5 to rip open the central file.
In the UI, this would show up as an "Attacking Repertory Book" suggesting the boldest theoretical gambit choices.
Approach B: Fine-Tuning $\Phi$ on Early-Game Tactical Tension
What it does: Right now, our $\Phi$-net was trained on 261,000 middlegame/endgame tactical puzzles. It learned that pins, forks, and back-rank threats create blunders.
The Opening Gap: In moves 1–8, back-rank mates don't exist yet! The danger comes from uncastled kings, open central files, and rapid lead in development.
If we train or fine-tune $\Phi$ on early sharp opening positions, $\Phi$ learns that having open c and d files against an uncastled king has a huge error multiplier, even before any tactical sacrifice has occurred!
2. The Trap to Avoid: Don't Limit It Only to the Danish Gambit
If we train a 542k-parameter ResNet on only Danish Gambit games:

The Dataset is too small: High-level master games in the Danish Gambit number in the hundreds or low thousands. A neural net will simply memorize the exact move orders rather than learning general principles of sacrificial attack.
Overfitting: The model would be a genius in the Danish, but completely blind in the Evans Gambit or the Sicilian Dragon.
3. The Better Strategy: The "Romantic / Dynamic Attack" Corpus
Instead of just one opening, we curate a focused dataset of High-Initiative / Gambit Openings:

Open King's Pawn Gambits: Danish Gambit, Evans Gambit, King's Gambit, Scotch Gambit, Göring Gambit.
Semi-Open Sharpness: Smith-Morra Gambit, Sicilian Dragon / Najdorf Yugoslav Attack, French Winawer.
Queen's Pawn & Flank Attacks: Blackmar-Diemer, Benko Gambit, King's Indian Mar del Plata.
How to filter the games:

High-rated human master games (2200+ FIDE / Lichess) or TCEC / engine gambit tournaments.
Filter for games with high dynamic tension (material sacrificed within the first 12 moves, decisive results).
4. What This Gives the UI: A "Tal / Romantic Opening Mode"
Imagine having a selector right above your board:

🧊 Classical Engine Mode: Plays safe, solid positional openings (QGD, Berlin, Slav).
⚡ Tal / Romantic Opening Mode: Prioritizes active piece play, gambits, and early king hunts. When you play 1. e4 e5, it immediately highlights:
2. d4 exd4 3. c3 (Danish Gambit)
2. f4 (King's Gambit)
With full variation branches and predicted opponent blunder rates!
